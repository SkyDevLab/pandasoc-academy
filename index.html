◉01
TRIAGE

Choose what matters

Understand why severity alone is not enough. Prioritise with asset criticality, identity privileges, chronology, detection confidence and possible business impact.

What triage is for

Triage is the first structured decision: which alert deserves attention now, what evidence supports that decision, and who needs to know? It is not a final verdict on the entire incident.

Read the alert in context

  • Asset: Is the host a domain controller, production server, employee laptop or low-impact test machine?
  • Identity: Is the account a standard user, administrator, service identity or privileged emergency account?
  • Baseline: Is this activity normal for this user, host, time and workload?
  • Sequence: Are there related events immediately before or after the alert?
  • Confidence: Which event fields support the detection, and what is still unknown?
🐼 Panda tip: write one sentence explaining why this alert is prioritised. “Critical server + unusual privileged logon + correlated process activity” is more useful than “looks bad.”

⌕02
VALIDATION & SCOPE

Follow the evidence

Separate a true detection from a true incident. Build a timeline, pivot between entities, and document both supporting and contradicting evidence.

Three different questions

  • Alert validation: does the underlying telemetry support what the detection claims?
  • Incident determination: does the validated activity represent unauthorised or harmful activity under the organisation's criteria?
  • Scope: which identities, endpoints, applications, data and time periods may be connected?

Build an evidence timeline

Normalize timestamps and note the timezone. Record the earliest known event, the trigger, follow-on activity and any containment action. Preserve the raw event or stable reference so another analyst can reproduce the conclusion.

⌁03
INDICATORS

Spot the clues

Distinguish IOCs from behaviour, correlate weak signals, and understand why context and detection coverage matter.

IOC, behaviour and hypothesis

An indicator of compromise (IOC) is an observable artefact that may be associated with malicious activity: for example, a file hash, domain, IP address, file path or suspicious account. An IOC is not automatically malicious in every environment.

An indicator of attack (IOA) emphasizes an action or behaviour, such as repeated authentication attempts followed by an unusual successful sign-in. A TTP describes tactics, techniques and procedures: patterns in how an adversary pursues an objective.

Observed clueCategoryNext useful question
SHA-256 value for a suspicious fileIOC / artefactWhere else was the file observed?

Think beyond a single signal

Correlate identity, endpoint, network and cloud evidence where available. A signed administrative tool can be legitimate; the same tool used at an unusual time by a compromised account may deserve closer investigation.

Panda reminder: indicators decay. Record where a clue came from, when it was observed, and the confidence you have in its meaning.

⬡04
CONTAINMENT

Act without losing control

Understand containment trade-offs, approvals, evidence preservation and why the right response depends on incident context.

Containment is a risk decision

Containment aims to limit further harm while preserving the ability to investigate and restore. The response could involve isolating an endpoint, disabling a compromised account, revoking sessions, blocking a known malicious indicator, or restricting a service.

  • Use the organisation's incident plan, authority boundaries and escalation path.
  • Consider business criticality, safety, dependencies and the impact of isolation.
  • Capture volatile or easily lost evidence where feasible and authorized before disruptive actions.
  • Record who approved the action, when it happened, what changed and how rollback will work.
  • Do not rely on a single indicator block when the underlying identity or host may remain compromised.
⚠️

Do not improvise on a live incident. These are educational examples, not step-by-step instructions to change a real production environment. Follow your organization's playbook and obtain the required authorization.

RECOVERY

Restore, then verify

Recovery is more than reconnecting a machine. Validate eradication, access, monitoring and service health before declaring closure.

A deliberate path back

  • Eradication: address the confirmed root cause, persistence or compromised access as directed by the response team.
  • Validation: check that the suspected entry path and related indicators are addressed.
  • Restore: use approved known-good configurations, backups or rebuilds as appropriate.
  • Verify: confirm service health, identity control, logging and monitoring after restoration.
  • Watch: increase monitoring for recurrence, re-entry or new related activity.

Keep the business owner and incident lead informed. A restored service is not proof that every attacker foothold has been removed; closure should follow the organisation's defined criteria.

▤06
REPORTING & LEARNING

Tell the story clearly

Produce a useful incident record that separates facts, analysis, decisions, impact, cost assumptions and follow-up work.

A clear incident record

  • Summary: what happened, when, and the current status.
  • Evidence: source, timestamp/timezone, identifier, preservation location and relevant excerpt.
  • Assessment: what the evidence supports, what it does not prove and remaining uncertainty.
  • Scope and impact: affected systems, identities, information and business functions.
  • Actions: containment/recovery steps, approvals, outcomes and exceptions.
  • Follow-up: control gaps, detection improvements, owners and due dates.

Estimate cost transparently. Separate direct costs (response labour or specialist services), indirect costs (interruption and lost productivity) and consequential costs (such as downstream legal or customer effects). State the data source and assumptions; do not present unknown amounts as established facts.

The best report lets another analyst reconstruct the decision without depending on the original analyst's memory.

THE RESPONSE PLAYBOOK

A calm sequence
when the alert is noisy.

Use this as a mental model, not a replacement for your team's policy. Real investigations may revisit earlier steps as new evidence arrives.

01

Receive and record

Capture the alert ID, source, timestamp and timezone, detection logic, affected entity and initial severity. Note who owns the investigation.

02

Validate the detection

Inspect the underlying event and relevant telemetry. Identify the key supporting evidence and look for benign explanations without assuming one.

03

Preserve and scope

Follow evidence-handling policy, identify related identities/endpoints and build a timeline. Record collection sources and gaps.

04

Assess severity and impact

Use the organisation's classification matrix. Consider business criticality, information exposure, functional disruption and recoverability.

05

Escalate and contain

Engage incident leadership and system owners where required. Use authorized controls and document the risk trade-offs and action times.

06

Eradicate, recover, verify

Address the root cause, restore through approved procedures, confirm service and security telemetry, then monitor for recurrence.

07

Close the loop

Record the timeline, decisions, impact, confidence and follow-up actions. Feed lessons into detections, playbooks and training.

OBSERVABLE ARTEFACT

File hash

A cryptographic digest can help identify a specific file. It does not by itself prove the file executed, caused harm, or is malicious in every context.

Good follow-up questions

  • Where was the file found and collected?
  • Was it executed, and by which process or user?
  • Do endpoint controls or sandbox results add context?

DEFENDER TAKEAWAY

Don't rely on one clue.

A hash can be useful for identifying a known sample but changing a file can change its hash. Infrastructure may also change. Behavioural detections can be more durable, although they require good telemetry and careful tuning to avoid false positives.

Panda says: match the signal to the question you're trying to answer.

FIELD NOTES

Small habits.
Stronger investigations.

These are reminders for clear thinking, evidence quality and handoffs between analysts.

01 / Facts vs. inference

Label the difference

“The log records a successful login” is a fact about a source. “The account was compromised” is a conclusion that needs additional support.

02 / Time matters

Normalize timestamps

Record timezone and time source. A timeline built from mixed local and UTC timestamps can invent ordering that did not happen.

03 / Evidence matters

Keep it reproducible

Capture source references, relevant fields, collection time and hashes where appropriate under policy. Do not edit the only original evidence copy.

04 / Containment matters

Coordinate changes

Blocking, disabling or isolating can reduce harm but may disrupt service and change evidence. Use approvals and the designated playbook.

05 / Reporting matters

State what is unknown

Document telemetry gaps, unverified scope and assumptions. A transparent uncertainty is more useful than false certainty.

06 / Recovery matters

Monitor for recurrence

Validate service health and security controls against closure criteria, then use lessons learned to improve monitoring and procedures.

Panda's frequently asked questions

Does every alert mean there is an incident?

No. An alert is a detection or lead. Validate the underlying event and apply your organization's incident criteria before labelling the activity.

Should I always isolate a suspicious machine immediately?

Not as a universal rule. Containment depends on evidence, business impact, risk, authority and your incident response plan. Escalate and coordinate when required.

Can one IP address prove compromise?

Usually not by itself. Consider direction, service, timing, reputation, expected traffic, endpoint evidence and other independent signals.

What if the logs I need are missing?

Record the gap, determine whether another authorized telemetry source exists, note retention or collection limitations, and make conclusions proportional to available evidence.

Is this an official incident-response standard?

No. PandaSOC Academy is an educational learning aid. Follow your organisation's approved policies, incident handling plan and applicable reporting requirements.
